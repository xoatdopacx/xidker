12 APR 2026

Your build is a user interface.

2 MIN READ

A developer starts a build. The screen says nothing for forty seconds. Then it prints six hundred lines and exits with a number. We would never ship this experience to a customer. Somehow, when the customer is another engineer, we call it tooling.

A build has all the things a user interface has: a person trying to do something, a system making decisions, and a gap between the two. Progress, good defaults, useful feedback, and a clear next step matter just as much here.

My favourite improvement to Latchline wasn’t the cache. It was a single line that explained why a task was running. “Rebuilding because the shared schema changed.” An extra sentence, and suddenly the system felt like a colleague instead of a locked door.

Measure build time, certainly. But also notice how often someone opens the output, sighs, and asks a teammate what happened. That’s your other performance metric.

25 MAR 2026

The underrated art of a useful error.

2 MIN READ

Invalid configuration. Technically true. Emotionally unhelpful. The computer knows which field is wrong, what it expected, and where it looked. It could tell us. It has chosen mystery.

A useful error does three things: says what happened, explains what the system needed, and offers the smallest reasonable next step. Not a stack trace instead of a sentence. Not an apology with no directions.

In Pocketworks, I treat errors as part of the public interface. They get reviewed alongside function signatures. The wording is tested. File paths are real. Examples are copyable. When a fix is safe and obvious, the tool offers it rather than quietly making the decision.

Someone will meet your error at the end of a long day. You don’t need to be clever. You need to be on their side.

FILED UNDER: SMALL DETAILS / RH.

08 FEB 2026

A small case for boring software.

2 MIN READ

“Boring” is an unfair word for a remarkable achievement: a system that behaves the way people expect, for long enough that they stop worrying about it.

There is nothing unimaginative about getting there. It takes curiosity to understand the real problem, restraint to avoid solving five imaginary ones, and a particular kind of care to handle the uninteresting edge cases.

I’m not arguing against new ideas. I’m arguing for spending the novelty budget where it changes someone’s day. A new scheduling algorithm might be exactly right. A new configuration language probably needs a stronger argument than “it was fun to write.”

The best compliment I’ve received about a piece of infrastructure was, “Oh, I forgot we used that.” It had quietly worked for eleven months. I’m still quite proud of that.

CASE STUDY 01 / LATCHLINE

BUILD INFRASTRUCTURE / 2025

Build less.
Get on with more.

Latchline is a dependency-aware build orchestrator for Morrowfield’s growing codebase. It does the work that matters, explains why, and leaves the rest alone.

Partner
Morrowfield
My part
Architecture, implementation, rollout
Under the hood
Rust · Content-addressed caching

The friction

Every change rebuilt almost everything. Engineers waited 18 minutes and 42 seconds on an average run, and nobody quite trusted a green result. The existing scripts had outgrown the team that first wrote them.

The quieter way

Map the dependencies. Cache the actual inputs. Make every decision explainable. We introduced the new runner one workspace at a time, with a reversible migration and a readable record of what changed.

Average pipeline time: 5 minutes, 16 seconds. More importantly, engineers could finally explain what their build was doing.

CASE STUDY 02 / THREADWORK

DEVELOPER EXPERIENCE / 2024

Follow the thread.
Find the thing.

Threadwork turns scattered service events into one readable request journey. Built with the people who have to work out what happened at an inconvenient hour.

Partner
Stillmere
My part
Research, product engineering, SDK
Under the hood
Go · TypeScript · Event streams

The friction

Sixty-two services, four different log formats, and a lot of carefully maintained browser tabs. Finding a slow request meant asking three teams and matching timestamps by hand.

The quieter way

A small instrumentation library, a consistent event shape, and a timeline that speaks in the team’s language. We designed around real incidents, not dashboard screenshots. Every hop points to the owner and the relevant context.

One request. One connected story. The most useful feature turned out to be a shareable view that didn’t need a ten-minute explanation.

INDEPENDENT TOOLS / 2023—ONGOING

A smaller
kind of useful.

Pocketworks is an ongoing collection of little command-line tools. Each one starts with a repeated annoyance and ends, ideally, before it needs a configuration file.

Format
Small, single-purpose utilities
My part
The whole small thing
Under the hood
Rust · Shell · Plain text

Inside the toolbox

envfold compares environment files without exposing their secrets. waitwell waits for a service and tells you what it’s waiting for. pathnote leaves a small, searchable explanation next to a complicated directory.

Small on purpose

Readable help text. Predictable exit codes. A dry-run option before anything destructive. Each tool is designed to be understood in a minute and quietly useful for years.

A tool doesn’t need a platform strategy to earn its place in someone’s pocket.
